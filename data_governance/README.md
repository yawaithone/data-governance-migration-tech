## What this report is about

As of April 2025, over 122 million people are forcibly displaced worldwide. Governments, NGOs,
and corporations increasingly manage this migration using biometric data, surveillance, and
predictive analytics. These systems promise stronger border security and efficiency — but their
ethical and governance frameworks have not kept pace, creating real risks for some of the most
vulnerable people in society.

This report analyses that tension through two real-world anchors:

- **The 2024 Greek ruling** — the Hellenic Data Protection Authority fined the Ministry of
  Migration and Asylum for operating the *Kentauros* and *Hyperion* surveillance systems without
  proper Data Protection Impact Assessments (DPIAs), exposing deep gaps in accountability.
- **The EU–US Enhanced Border Security Partnership (EBSP)** — a proposal granting U.S. agencies
  real-time biometric access to core EU databases (EURODAC, SIS, VIS, EES, ETIAS, ECRIS-TCN),
  raising serious concerns about GDPR compliance, transparency, and redress.

Everything is evaluated against **SDG 10 (Reduced Inequalities)** and **SDG 16 (Peace, Justice
and Strong Institutions)**, with **SDG 17** touched on for cross-border cooperation.

## Key arguments

- **The problem isn't a lack of law — it's a lack of enforcement.** The EU has some of the
  strongest data protection frameworks in the world (GDPR, EU Charter of Fundamental Rights, EU
  AI Act), yet ethical implementation remains weak and inconsistent, especially across borders.
- **Consent can become coercion.** When migrants must surrender biometric data to access food,
  shelter, or legal aid, "freely given consent" under GDPR Articles 6, 7, and 9 loses its meaning.
- **Compliance is not enough.** DPIAs should be paired with **Ethical Impact Assessments (EIAs)**
  that ask whether a system upholds human dignity, fairness, and the common good.
- **Governance must be human-centred and multi-stakeholder** — involving independent oversight
  bodies (EDPS, EU-LISA), NGOs, and organisations like the UNHCR, not just states and law
  enforcement.

## Ethical frameworks applied

The report evaluates biometric migration technologies through four ethical lenses:

- **Utilitarian** — efficiency, fraud detection, and border security benefits
- **Deontological** — duties around privacy, consent, and moral obligation (Kantian ethics)
- **Virtue ethics** — what character and values an institution embeds into its systems
- **Rawls' veil of ignorance** — fairness that ignores ethnicity, gender, and socio-economic status

---

## Recommendations (selected)

- Embed privacy and ethics **by design**, from collection through to deletion
- Conduct **independent bias audits** of algorithmic systems before deployment
- Enforce **data minimization** and strict purpose limitation
- Use **advanced encryption** (in transit and at rest), strict access controls, and audit trails
- Provide **accessible, multilingual** channels for consent, correction, and complaint
- Establish an **EU Migration Data Ethics Board** issuing periodic transparency reports
- Deliver ongoing **staff training** and maintain **incident response plans**
